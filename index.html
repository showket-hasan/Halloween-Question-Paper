The Historical and Cultural Evolution of Halloween

Official Source Documentation & Text Analysis

Reading Passage Instructions

You should spend about 20 minutes on Questions 1–20, which are based on the Reading Passage below. Answer all questions grounded strictly in the provided text.

Paragraph A In contemporary North America, Halloween is widely recognized as a vibrant celebration characterized by elaborate disguises, social gatherings, and door-to-door trick-or-treating. For children, the highlight of October 31st consists of dressing up in imaginative attire—ranging from fairy-tale princesses to animals—and visiting neighborhood houses to collect complimentary candy by uttering the phrase 'trick or treat.' Adults frequently participate by hosting themed costume parties. However, beneath this modern surface of lighthearted entertainment lies a rich cultural lineage connected to ancient spiritual beliefs and cross-cultural rituals of ancestral remembrance, comparable in scope to Japan's Obon festival or Mexico's Day of the Dead (Día de los Muertos).

Paragraph B The historical origin of Halloween traces back approximately 2,000 years to the ancient Celtic people, who inhabited regions comprising present-day Ireland, Scotland, and northern France. The Celts celebrated a festival known as Samhain (pronounced 'Sah-wvin'), marking All Hallows' Eve on October 31st. Celtic tradition held that on this specific night, the boundary separating the living from the deceased became porous, allowing spirits of the dead to return to Earth. While benevolent ancestral spirits were welcomed, harmful or evil entities were believed to roam the land as well. To safeguard their souls from being stolen or harmed by wandering evil spirits, the Celts wore disguises constructed from animal skins.

Paragraph C Over subsequent centuries, the ancient practice of wearing animal skins evolved into the modern custom of wearing fancy dress costumes. Similarly, door-to-door trick-or-treating underwent a major historical shift. Following the Celtic period, a custom emerged wherein individuals visited nearby households to offer prayers for the deceased ancestors of the residents. In exchange for these spiritual prayers, wealthier hosts would distribute food to the visitors, particularly to poorer members of the community. In contemporary society, 'trick' signifies a practical joke or prank, while 'treat' refers to a sweet snack or confection. For modern children, the religious obligation has dissolved, leaving a popular ritual of receiving free treats.

Paragraph D Another enduring emblem of Halloween is the Jack-O'-Lantern, a carved pumpkin illuminated from within. According to Celtic folklore, the term originates from a clever man named Jack who made a pact with the devil, selling his soul but later tricking the devil to avoid his fate. Upon his death, Jack was denied entry into heaven and condemned to wander the Earth in darkness. He was granted a single burning ember or coal from hellfire to light his path. Jack placed this glowing coal inside a hollowed-out turnip—a common root vegetable in Ireland and the United Kingdom. When Irish and British immigrants arrived in North America, turnips were scarce, prompting them to adapt the tradition by carving orange pumpkins, which were native and abundant in the New World.

Paragraph E In addition to official Halloween festivities on October 31st, informal traditions have also surfaced. Among these is 'Devil's Night,' an unofficial observance taking place on October 30th, the evening prior to Halloween. Unconnected to ancient Celtic religion, Devil's Night traditionally involves youths engaging in light mischief and vandalism, such as draping toilet paper over buildings or smashing carved pumpkins. Despite these localized disruptions, Halloween remains every October 31st as a globally recognized event that blends ancient Celtic heritage, folk tales of Jack and his turnip lantern, and modern festive revelry.
